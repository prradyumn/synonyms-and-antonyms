Reef See-saw — sea-world antonym game

Ten screens. A six-second opening beat, the round loop with all three outcomes, the scaffold fade, and the ending. The tilt is the mechanic and the rocking is the reward.

The whole design in one line. A see-saw is already an opposites machine — one end can only go up if the other goes down. So don't invent physics around it. Put a word on each seat and let the see-saw do the teaching.

Opposites make it rock. Two different riders tip and swing: up, down, up, down. The see-saw works, and the rocking itself repeats the pair aloud.
Same-meaning words make it stick. Two of the same weight sit dead level and nothing happens. Both riders get bored. That is the near-miss, and it is legible without a word of explanation.
An unrelated word won't board at all. The rider takes one look, turns round and swims off.

The cast

Pedagogy: three tilts, three relationships

What the see-saw doesWord relationshipWhat is being taught
Rocks up and downtrue oppositeOpposites are a pair that move against each other. The child hears "up, down, up, down" while watching it happen — the reward animation is the drill.
Sticks dead levelsame meaningThe near-miss. Two words that mean the same are the same weight, so there is no difference to make anything move. Nothing is marked wrong; it is simply boring.
Rider refuses to boardunrelatedSome words aren't in the conversation at all. Separating "wrong but related" from "wrong and unconnected" is a real step in word knowledge.

Each round offers exactly one of each, shuffled. That is the whole item design — and it makes every attempt diagnostic, because which wrong answer a child reaches for says more than whether they were right.

Pairs, never single words. The summary shows what was learned as linked pairs with the arrow between them, and speaks each one a final time. Three shells for finishing, not for accuracy — a child who needed every retry still leaves with three.

SPOKENHot and cold. Big and small. Fast and slow. WHYOne free repetition on the way out costs four seconds and is the cheapest retention gain in the design.

The rocking beat, frame by frame

TimeWhat happensWhy
0–80 msRider lands on the empty seat with a soft thud and a puff of sandAny touch without a response inside 100 ms and a six-year-old taps harder
80–400 msThe plank breaks free of the sand and swings past levelThe release from being stuck is the moment of success — it must be felt, not announced
400–2200 msFour full rocks. Narration calls "up! down! up! down!" on the beatThe reward animation is the drill. Four free repetitions of the direction words
2200–2800 msSwing settles; both words spoken as a pair, then the relationship namedPairing the two words aloud is the actual retention mechanism
2800 msChime, pitched up one step per consecutive correct answerA wordless streak meter that needs no interface at all

All art here is placeholder SVG drawn for this sheet — Otto, Snip, Puff, Fin, Glow, the rock fulcrum, coral, kelp and shells. Every spoken line is pre-recorded human VO in the build, never text-to-speech: when the word being taught is the word being mispronounced, the cost is higher than usual.

Screens 03 and 04 are the pair to playtest first. Screen 03 has to feel like a real see-saw ride or the whole reward collapses; screen 04 has to read as boring rather than as failure. If children flinch at the dead-level state instead of laughing at it, the no-fail premise needs rethinking before any production art is commissioned.
